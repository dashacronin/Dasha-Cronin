I've spent over 12 years in health tech, and what keeps me here is the mission to make healthcare better and more accessible for people. Behind that is something more personal. My family came to this country as immigrants and had a difficult road navigating the healthcare system. Watching them figure it out is part of what drives me.

In the last six years I found a calling in mental health specifically. Working in support of providers has made me feel close to the real impact this work can have. Working on the patient side would make it feel full circle.

Professionally, what energizes me is the fuzzy middle. When a problem is real but still undefined, and someone needs to figure out what we are actually solving for. That is where I do my best work: tinkering with the tradeoffs, figuring out what we are willing to do to solve it, and building the execution plan to go from zero to one and iterate from there.

Why Headway

The access problem is real

Mental healthcare has a supply problem, a navigation problem, and an affordability problem — often all at once. Headway is solving all three simultaneously, and doing it by making the provider side of the equation actually work at scale.

Where I've built

I've spent five-plus years building the operational infrastructure that keeps behavioral health providers engaged, retained, and delivering great care. When providers are well-supported, patients get access to the care they need.

Maxwell and PatientPing shaped how I think

Before Lyra, I was at PatientPing, a care coordination platform built around improving the patient journey across different points of care, and at Maxwell Health, where I was building for the end user — the employee or future patient — thinking about how to make a benefit plan understandable to an average person and how to increase literacy around what their insurance actually means. Those experiences shaped how I think about the member side of the equation: operational decisions made upstream ripple directly into whether a person can actually access the care they need.

The mission is personal

I've seen firsthand the difference between people who get access to mental healthcare and people who don't. The barriers are often invisible — cultural stigmas that make it hard to even name the need, access challenges that make it hard to find care even when the need is clear. I want to continue being part of the equation that helps solve this. Not because it's a good professional story, but because I think it's one of the most important health layers we need to get right as a society.

What I'd bring in the first 90 days

I'd start by getting deeply oriented — understanding the operational landscape, the team, and the real friction points in the patient experience before building anything. Then I'd focus on the same things I've built before: clear ownership frameworks, operating cadences that surface risk early, and the kind of accountability infrastructure that makes a complex, distributed operation actually run. And I'd look immediately for where AI and automation can reduce manual overhead so the team can focus on the decisions that actually need a human.
